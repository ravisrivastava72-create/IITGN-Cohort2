# Which letters build the complete definition of the number the scheme pays for?

Answers: 1c 2b 3d 4a

*Illustrative case: the company is real, the situation and the data are synthetic, built for this class.*

Uber's Hyderabad city team launched a peak-hour guarantee on 31 August 2026: Rs 300 per peak online
hour for an enrolled driver who stays online through a whole peak window, with Uber topping up the
gap. Six weeks later peak online hours were up 24 percent, completed peak trips up 3 percent, and
trips per peak online hour down from 1.90 to 1.58, with Rs 1.9 crore paid in top-up. The room chose
a new north star, completed peak trips per enrolled driver online at peak per week, and this set
built its definition one line at a time.

## What does the set test about a definition you can pay against?

A number becomes safe to pay against when it is written down in seven lines, and each of the four
items fixes one line that the old scheme left open. The old scheme paid for an hour with the app
open, so the drivers who sat online in quiet zones were paid the same as those who drove. The
finished definition reads 35.76 trips per online driver-week before the launch and 35.89 after:
hours rose 24 percent and the number the scheme exists for barely moved.

| Line | The definition the room built |
|---|---|
| North star | Completed peak trips per enrolled driver online at peak, per week |
| Numerator | Trips completed inside the peak windows, one per paying rider |
| Denominator | Enrolled drivers online at least one peak hour that week, the count printed beside the rate |
| Window | The calendar week |
| Exclusions | Trips cancelled by either side, test accounts, trips outside the windows, each change dated |
| What it does not reward | Hours online; offers declined; a shrinking enrolled base, since fewer drivers in the count would raise the rate with no extra trip |
| Guardrail 1, for riders | Offers accepted over offers shown, at or above an acceptance floor the city sets, read with the share of accepted trips the driver cancels |
| Guardrail 2, for riders | Median rider wait at peak |
| Guardrail 3, for the budget | Top-up per completed trip |
| Gaming note | Guardrail 1 catches declining and cherry-picking, and the cancel share beside it accepting then cancelling; guardrail 2 catches supply that stopped serving riders; guardrail 3 catches a top-up that runs away from the trips it pays for |

## Which item is worth arguing about?

Item 2, option c. "Drivers who completed at least one peak trip" sounds like the honest choice,
because it counts only drivers who really drove. It is the trap of every denominator defined by the
outcome: a driver who logs in and completes nothing leaves the base, so the rate looks best exactly
when drivers stop doing the work. The denominator has to be set by something other than the
outcome it divides, which is why the room chose "online at least one peak hour" and printed the count beside it.

## Where does a written definition show up at work?

Swiggy's shareholder letters carry a glossary that writes each metric down with its numerator,
denominator and window: platform frequency, for example, counts completed orders per transacting
user, per month, averaged over the period, and it read 4.53 in the quarter to 30 September 2024,
beside 17.1 million monthly transacting users
(Swiggy's Q2 FY25 shareholder letter, filed with BSE, formerly the Bombay Stock Exchange, checked 12
October 2026). A reader can rebuild the number and see what it leaves out: its denominator, users
who ordered, is set by ordering itself, the trap of item 2, which is why the letter prints that count
beside the rate. The SEC's 2020 order
against Wells Fargo shows the other side: its cross-sell ratio counted accounts and products whether
or not anyone used them, a numerator with no usage condition (SEC order, Release 34-88257, checked
12 October 2026).
